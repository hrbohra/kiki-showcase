The screen where trust is actually made

Kiki isn't selling rooms. It's selling permission to let a stranger sleep in your bed. Every other screen either builds that permission or spends it, but exactly one screen has to carry it: the moment someone reads a request and decides yes or no.

Five versions below, newest first: the one built in your product's own language, the layered synthesis, and the three display models those came out of. Same person, same evidence, same synthetic world your demo already ships. They differ in what they ask the human to do with it.

One screen, two anxieties. The host is asking will they respect my home. The guest is asking will I be safe in theirs. Same graph, same guest book, different load-bearing question, so the copy and the gap statement flip.
Four rules every version holds to
No trust score. A person is not a number. The rank badge in the current build is the one thing I removed on purpose.
Every claim is checkable. Transparency you can't verify isn't transparency, it's persuasion.
Gaps get the same weight as evidence. Not greyed out, not a footnote.
The mutual friend is a person, not a citation. Nina is being named to broker a deal she isn't in. That needs her consent and it needs to route back to her.

Your product already has a way of saying “a real person said this”. It's the mint bubble inside the teal card. I didn't need to invent a marker, I needed to give it more to say.

Grouped by how you'd judge it

The scroll used to alternate between kinds of thinking: behavioural proof, then a human vouch, then an algorithmic badge, then abstract graph data, then synthesis. Every switch costs the reader a mode change. It's now three groups, each one evaluation mode: her track record (what she's actually done, and who wrote about it), the people behind her (every human endorsement plus the paths they sit on), and what we think, and what we don't know (everything algorithmic, kept last and kept together). Enclosure and proximity do the grouping, so no new chrome was needed.

Weight follows meaning, not enthusiasm

Nina's card was solid teal and outweighed Maia's own face. It's now pale mint with a teal edge strip, so the heaviest ink on the card belongs to the correction: Nina's talking about Iris here, not about Maia. That reordering is the product's argument in one component. The correction went the other way in the same pass. It was a solid dark slab, which made Kiki interrupt in a louder voice than the person it was correcting. It's now white with a 3px brick strip down its left edge, and the what we don't know card carries the identical strip instead of the thin brick border it used to have. One mark, one meaning, in both places a caveat appears, so a caveat reads as a caveat rather than as emphasis.

Tie strength, not just distance

The two routes used to be listed a second time under their own heading, right below the vouch cards that already named Nina and Sophie. Same two people, two containers, twice the scroll. Each route now lives on its own vouch card, so the section beneath is only the picture, and the words and the geometry stop competing. Granovetter's point is that a weak tie and a strong tie are different objects, not different lengths. Tie strength also has a channel of its own now. Nina has stayed with you twice, so her card shows three filled bars and her line in the graph is thick. You and Sophie have been at one Kiki picnic, so hers is one bar and a thin line. Solid versus dashed keeps meaning something else entirely, whether that person left a note, so the two variables can disagree: a close friend who wrote nothing draws thick and dashed, which is exactly what happened. Both readings come from data you already hold, stay history and event attendance, never a guess about closeness.

Same reasoning fixed the button. Naming three people to ask invites nobody to be asked, so it names one and says why: she's the one who's hosted for you before. One clear path beats a menu of equals.

The butler, not the stalker

“You were both at the Dalston picnic in June” read as surveillance when it arrived unasked. It's now folded behind 3 things we spotted about you two, and opening it is the user's move. The subtitle moved too: nobody told us put the emphasis on Kiki knowing things it wasn't told, which is the sentence a stalker would write. It reads none of this came from a person, here's how we worked it out, which offers the method instead of boasting about the reach. The facts are identical and each still carries how we know it. Same data, no defensive reaction, because the person chose to look.

The mechanics paragraph moved behind an i for the same reason. It's the best line on the screen for a pitch and pure scroll cost for someone deciding at 11pm.

The state that decides the company

The screen beside this one is the cold match: no mutuals, nothing in common, a stranger asking for your keys. Almost every trust design skips it, and in a new city it's the majority case. It opens by saying so plainly, then gives what's actually there, an invite from someone four steps out and two entries from strangers, and three ways forward that aren't yes or no: an introduction, a call, or one night instead of three. Trustworthy and untrustworthy newcomers look identical on day one, so the honest move is to make the first step smaller rather than imply a confidence nobody has earned.

Measured, not eyeballed

A review of this screen flagged the helper text under each route as failing AA. I measured it: #2C6559 on mint is 5.98:1 and passes comfortably. The real failure was somewhere else entirely, and it was inkFaint, the token doing most of the small-caption work: #8A9099 on white is 3.21:1, so every 12px caption on the screen failed, including “asked you 2 days ago” and every provenance line under the inferences. Lifted to #626C75 at 4.84:1 in 114 places. The baseline recreation keeps the original value, because it is a faithful copy and should show what the live app actually renders.

That is two contrast findings now that came from measuring rather than looking, both in the same token file. Worth a single pass over tokens.ts with a contrast checker before the next screen gets built on it.

The diagram has a text twin

Rings are hostile to a screen reader, so the SVG is aria-hidden and sits beside a visually hidden list that states the same four facts in order: where Nina is and what she wrote, where Sophie is and that she wrote nothing, that Maia is two steps out reached both ways, and why nothing past three steps is drawn. Voice-over users get the argument, not an apology for a picture.

The nodes were 34px, under the 44pt floor, so each now sits in a 44px target that opens that person's profile, and the peripheral stub lines and dots came out, since they encoded nothing. Cards went from 12px to 16px padding, and inner blocks all settled on a 12px radius against the card's 18px.

The footer was the other note, and my first attempt at it made things worse: putting the reason inline but keeping a 9px padding on top of a 44px floor grew the button from 61px to 64px. It is now exactly 44px, the touch floor and nothing more, with the strip padding tightened, which takes the whole footer from 159px to about 135px, roughly 15% of the phone rather than 18%. Worth stating plainly because I claimed the reduction before I had measured it.

Motion, and where I stopped

The tab indicator glides and settles under Trust on mount, buttons scale to 0.97 on press, disclosures fade up 6px, and all of it collapses to near zero under prefers-reduced-motion.

Three suggestions I'd argue against, and I'd rather say so than quietly skip them. A typing reveal fakes computation on data that was already computed, and it delays the gap block, the one thing that must never arrive late. A pulsing recently-active ring makes presence look like trustworthiness; being online at 11pm says nothing about how someone treats a home. A 3D room preview is a good idea in the wrong tab: the room lives one tab left, and an orbitable model here splits the Trust tab's single job and buries the vouch. Give me the Room tab and it's the first thing I'd build.

Words users actually use

Two labels here said with words and link only. That's the database talking: one vouch row has a note on it, the other is a bare edge. A host reading that learns nothing. They now say Nina left a note and Sophie left no note, named person, concrete artefact, and Nina's note is sitting right above it so you can check the claim against itself. She said why was an improvement but still vague, because it named neither the person nor the thing. Chains became mutuals, a word people already use on Instagram. You → Nina → Maia became Through Nina, because the three faces beside it already draw the arrow. Never a guest became hasn't stayed anywhere yet, since the first reads like a verdict on her and the second is just a fact about the record.

The voice is the one in the Instagram archive: second person, contractions, no dashes, occasional exclamation, and Kiki says we. “We met Maia for coffee before we launched in London. One of the first hundred!” is a sentence a person wrote. “Kiki's own record · Jul 2025” was a database field with a date on it.

How this decomposes

Four components, each with one job, all reading ConnectionStory and the guest-book roll-up you already compute.

VouchCardCarries one human's endorsement. Variant is derived, not passed: worded when the vouch has a note, wordless when it doesn't, ops when the source is Kiki. The variant is what makes the weight legible, so it can never disagree with the data.
PathRowOne route through the graph. Takes a Member[] and renders faces plus a name. Needs allShortestPaths, not shortestPath, which is the one backend change on this screen.
InferenceListFacts nobody stated. Every row needs a source and a confidence, so the type is { claim, source, verified } and there's no way to render one without saying where it came from.
GapListWhat isn't known. Derived, never optional, and it renders even when the array is empty, because a screen that hides its gaps when convenient is worse than one that never had them.

Semantics follow from purpose. The disclosure is a real button with aria-expanded and a 44px target, not a tappable div. Nina's quote is a blockquote with a cite, because attribution is the whole point of it. Group titles are headings so the tab is navigable by rotor. The rings and the loop mark are aria-hidden, since every fact they encode is already stated in text above them.

Contrast pass

The badge on Nina's card was brandDark on brand, 1.3:1, invisible. It's now the loop mark reversed out of white. The correction line was white on teal at 12.5px, under 3:1, on the single most important sentence here; it measures 11.5:1 now on a deeper teal, which also makes it read as Kiki's own voice rather than Nina's. Every label sitting on a mint surface moved to #0E4E42.

Which surfaced something worth passing on. tokens.ts annotates brandDark as “text on brandTint (4.8:1 AA)”. Measured against brandTint it's 3.66:1, and fails. I'd taken the comment at its word in an earlier draft of this very page, which is the point: a wrong number in a token file propagates into every screen that trusts it, and nobody re-measures a comment. The baseline recreation below still uses the real value, unchanged.

Read off the live screensTeal is never decoration in your app, it appears only where a human vouches for a human. Dark pills carry dates. Outline pills carry counts. Frosted pills sit on photos. Everything else is white on near-white. That restraint is why the teal block lands, so every addition here stays inside those five moves.
Trust as a third tabRoom · Profile · Trust. It belongs in the IA you already have, next to the room and the person, not on a screen of its own. A host checks it before replying and never has to leave the profile.
The correction lives inside the teal card“Her words are about Iris, not about Maia” sits in Nina's own block, in white on teal. The platform corrects itself in the same breath as it makes the claim. Put that caveat in grey underneath and it reads as small print; put it inside the badge and it reads as integrity.
Three grades of the same componentFull teal when a person wrote words. Pale teal with a dashed strip when the vouch has no words. Plain white with an outline when it's a count rather than a voice. One component, three weights, and the weight itself tells you how much is behind it, no legend to learn.
Micro-touches worth keepingThe heart stays what it already means, save, in color.heart, nowhere near a trust claim. The bottom of the photo instead uses the caption line from ListingCard.tsx, with an honest pairing in it: hosted 3 times · never a guest. Pills, radii and the photo gradient are lifted from that same file rather than eyeballed. Dashed borders mean absence throughout, no words, no evidence, no proof you met. Deciding later is a real option.

Trust arrives the way it does in real life: somebody vouches for somebody, out loud, in a sentence.

Why it worksNarrative transportation: a story is processed as experience, not data, and it lowers perceived risk far faster than a list. Sociologically this is the oldest trust technology there is, the broker's word. Kiki's whole pitch is "your mutual friend in the city", so the interface should speak in that voice.
The typographic moveSerif means a human said it. Sans means the model inferred it. One rule, learned in two seconds, and it stops the AI from borrowing the credibility of a real person's words. That distinction is the difference between an introduction and a fabrication.
What it refusesThe primary action is not Accept. It's Ask Nina. The machine hands the decision back to the human broker instead of closing the deal itself, and "deciding later" is named, because a screen that only offers yes/no manufactures consent.
Where it failsFluent prose is persuasive whether or not it's true, so this is the variant most likely to produce over-reliance. It only survives ethically because every clause is tappable back to a source and the gap block is non-negotiable. If the model ever gets a hop wrong, this is the design that hides it best, the strongest argument for 1b.

Every claim carries who said it, when, and how far away they are. Nothing is summarised into a verdict.

Why it worksThe trust-calibration literature is blunt about this: transparency only helps when the user can actually verify the claim. If they can't, the same transparency becomes persuasion and produces auto-acceptance. So the unit here isn't a fact, it's a fact plus its provenance. "3 of the 4 reviewers are people you know" is the strongest row on the screen precisely because it is checkable by text message.
Why it's banded, not listedGrouping results by the property that matters beat a flat list at building trust in controlled studies. The bands are epistemic: someone you know said it → someone you don't → nobody said it, we inferred it → not known. Reading order is confidence order, so a skim is still calibrated.
The uncomfortable rowTheo's wifi complaint stays on screen. Two-sided evidence reads as more credible than uniform praise, and a platform that shows one flaw earns the right to be believed about everything else. The current build's sentiment meter would average it away into 82% Positive.
Where it failsIt costs the user real effort, and uncertainty information reliably increases workload. A tired host at 11pm wants 1a. My honest recommendation is that this is the layer underneath 1a, one tap down, the sentence for the decision, the receipt for the doubt.

Not a network diagram. A picture of social distance, with the honest edge of the network drawn in.

Why rings, not a graphThe finding that matters is that the fewer the degrees of separation, the more likely a stranger is read as in-group, and the more they're trusted. Distance is the variable, so distance should be the geometry. A force-directed hairball hides exactly the thing the host is trying to read.
The dashed line is the argumentPeer influence stops being meaningful at about three degrees. So the third ring is where the drawing stops and says so. Every other social product implies your network is infinite and infinitely meaningful; naming the boundary is what makes the inside of it credible.
Redundancy over proximityTwo independent chains is the sociologically stronger signal, and no current design surfaces it, your BFS returns one shortest path and stops. Cheap change, real gain: return all paths at the minimum length, then show how many.
Where it failsIt's the least useful of the three for a first-time host, because a diagram asks you to interpret before it tells you anything. It's also the one that leaks the most about people who never asked to be plotted, which is a consent problem, not a layout problem.
What I'd ship first

03 · The ten thousand conversations

What the Instagram archive is actually for

The manifesto is precise about it: two years of real back-and-forth, used to train the model in the exact tone and wording the mutual friend should have. Voice first. That framing is right, and it's narrower than people assume, so here is what I'd actually get out of it, in the order I'd do it.

01 · Voice
The screen should sound like the person who wrote those DMs.

Every sentence on 3a is written to be sayable by a friend, because that's the register the archive is in. This is the use Toby names, and it's the one that needs no new infrastructure, it's a style guide with ten thousand examples.

02 · The anxiety taxonomy
Two years of people telling you exactly what they're afraid of.

This is the most valuable thing in the archive for a trust interface, and it isn't facts, it's frequency. Which three questions come up before someone hands over keys decides what leads the screen and which gap must be named out loud. The What Kiki can't tell you block exists because those conversations keep asking the same things.

03 · Objection → resolution
What was said that turned a hesitant person into a host.

Those pairs are a copy library with proven conversion, not a brainstorm. Mined once, they become the microcopy for every reassurance on the screen, and each one can be traced back to the thread it came from, which is how you keep it honest.

04 · Ops memory into product data
The team already knows who was met for coffee. The archive is where that knowledge lives.

The Founding Kikier card works because a human remembered something true. Extracting those first-hand notes is how that card gets written for the next ten thousand members instead of the first hundred, with the sender's consent, and shown as Kiki's own record rather than a member's claim.

The line I wouldn't cross

A DM is a private conversation with Kiki, not a public statement about a third person. Voice generalises. Patterns generalise. Facts about individuals do not travel, the moment something Maia told you in confidence appears on a stranger's screen, the archive stops being an asset and becomes the reason people stop telling you things. Which is the whole company.

Practically: the corpus is a voice and priority asset before it is a fact asset. That's good news for the 2027 roadmap, because it means the mutual-friend model can be built on it without an inference engine that guesses about people, and guessing is the one failure a trust product cannot survive.
